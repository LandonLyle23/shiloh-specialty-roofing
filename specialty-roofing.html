Cedar Shake

Cedar shake grades, and why airflow matters more than the wood.

Cedar shake is graded before it reaches a roof. Grade #1 (Blue Label) is 100% clear heartwood, edge grain, tapersawn or hand-split, the grade specified for anything meant to last. Lower grades allow sapwood and flat grain, which cup, split, and rot years earlier. Ask for the grade by name, not just "cedar shake." Hand-split and taper-sawn are both legitimate; the choice is aesthetic, not a quality shortcut, as long as the grade is right.

Most cedar failures in North Georgia's humidity are airflow failures, not wood failures. Cedar has to dry on both faces between rain events. Nailed straight to solid decking with no gap, the underside never dries and shakes rot from below in half their rated life while looking fine from the ground. Correct installation uses skip sheathing or a cedar breather system so air moves under every course.

What goes wrong

Beyond missing ventilation: exposure stretched past spec to cut material use, dropping coverage from three layers to two; nails driven too high or low instead of concealed by the course above; no felt interlayment, letting wind-driven rain wick sideways under the shakes.

How to tell good work from bad

Straight courses, consistent exposure. Joints staggered at least 1.5" from the course above and below. A visible gap or breather mat under the shakes, not shakes laid flat on solid decking. Valleys and walls flashed per spec, not caulked.

Lifespan25 to 40 yrs
Biggest cost driverGrade + ventilation
Common failureRot from below

Standing Seam Metal

Standing seam versus exposed fastener. They are not the same roof

"Metal roof" covers two different products, and the price gap between them is not padding. Standing seam panels lock mechanically at raised seams and attach through concealed clips, so no fastener penetrates the panel face. Because the panel isn't pinned rigidly, it expands and contracts with temperature without stressing a fastener. Properly installed, it's routinely a 40 to 60 year roof.

Exposed fastener panels, ribbed, 5V, corrugated, are screwed directly through the face and sealed with rubber washers. They're a legitimate, lower-cost option, but every washer is a wear part. Georgia's UV and heat cycling crack them in roughly 10 to 15 years, turning hundreds of penetrations into potential leak points.

Ask directly: is the seam double-locked or snap-lock, and are there exposed fasteners anywhere on the panel field? Some products are marketed loosely as "standing seam" when they're closer to a snap-together look-alike. The answer should be specific.

What drives cost

Panel gauge and material (steel, aluminum, copper), seam type, finish system (Kynar/PVDF holds color far longer than baked enamel), and roof geometry. More hips, valleys, and penetrations mean more seams, trim, and labor per square.

What goes wrong

Panels fastened too rigidly with no allowance for thermal movement crack or oil-can. Dissimilar metals in contact, copper against steel for instance, set up galvanic corrosion that eats through the lesser metal over years. Clips spaced too far apart let panels rack in wind.

Lifespan40 to 60+ yrs
Biggest cost driverSeam type + gauge
Common failureWasher failure (exposed-fastener)

Natural Slate

Slate: the roof, and the roof underneath it, need to last the same amount of time

Quarried slate is graded largely by thickness and consistency. S1 grade is rated for 75 to 100+ years and is what's specified on a genuine restoration or high-end build. Slate is also the heaviest common roofing material, so the structure underneath, rafters and decking, often needs engineering review and reinforcement. That step is frequently missing from budget quotes, and it's not optional.

What actually determines whether a slate roof reaches its rated life is the flashing, not the slate. Slate this durable has to be flashed in copper; steel or aluminum will fail decades earlier, meaning sound slate gets torn up just to replace flashing that was never going to last as long as the stone around it.

What goes wrong

Galvanized nails instead of copper or stainless corrode and let slates slide loose within years, not decades. Nail holes punched too close to the edge crack the slate under thermal stress. Foot traffic during unrelated work cracks slates that go unnoticed until a leak shows up somewhere else entirely.

How to tell good work from bad

Consistent coursing and exposure across the roof. Copper flashing at every valley, chimney, and penetration, ask specifically what metal is used. Hip and ridge slates lapped and mechanically fastened. Nothing visibly slipped, cracked, or mismatched.

Lifespan75 to 100+ yrs
Biggest cost driverStructure + copper flashing
Common failureWrong nail / wrong flashing metal

Why slate quotes vary so much. Two bids for "the same roof" can differ by tens of thousands of dollars because one includes structural reinforcement and copper flashing throughout, and the other doesn't, until the framing inspection or a failed flashing at year twenty proves it.

Copper

Copper as a roof, and copper as the flashing that protects everything else

Full copper roofs are rare and genuinely premium: 16 to 20oz sheet, seamed or soldered, weathering from bright metal to brown patina to the familiar green over decades, with a service life that regularly exceeds a century. More often, copper's job is as flashing, valleys, chimneys, dormers, any penetration on a slate, tile, or premium cedar roof, because it's the one flashing metal with a lifespan that actually matches the material around it.

What drives cost

Copper is priced against a fluctuating commodity market, so material cost moves independently of labor. Soldered seam work, required for watertight valleys and built-in gutters, is a specialized, slow skill; far fewer people solder copper well than can run screws into a panel.

What goes wrong

Copper in direct contact with a less noble metal, aluminum gutters, galvanized flashing, steel fasteners, sets up galvanic corrosion that eats through the other metal. Long runs without expansion joints oil-can and split at the seams. Soldered joints done too quickly leave pinholes that won't show up as a leak for a season or two.

How to tell good work from bad

Solder lines are clean, continuous, smooth, no lumps or pinholes. Copper is isolated from dissimilar metals wherever they'd otherwise touch. Long runs show visible expansion detailing, not one rigid sheet.

Lifespan60 to 100+ yrs
Biggest cost driverCommodity price + solder labor
Common failureGalvanic corrosion
Clay & Concrete Tile

Tile: the underlayment is doing more work than the tile is

Clay tile is fired through, so its color is permanent, there's no coating to wear off. Concrete tile costs less but carries its color in a surface coating that fades over decades. Both are heavy, both routinely exceed 50 years, and both need the same structural check slate does.

What surprises most homeowners: tile isn't a sealed waterproof membrane, it's closer to a rain screen. It sheds the bulk of the water, but wind-driven rain gets underneath, and the underlayment beneath the tile is doing the actual waterproofing. A 50-year tile roof over cheap 15# felt fails at 15 to 20 years while the tile on top still looks brand new, meaning a full tear-off just to replace what's underneath it.

What drives cost

Structural verification, underlayment quality (self-adhered or high-grade synthetic vs. felt), battens vs. direct-to-deck mounting, and the complexity of hip and ridge detailing.

What goes wrong

Underlayment cut to hit a price point. Broken tiles from foot traffic left unrepaired. Ridge and hip tiles set in mortar or foam alone instead of mechanically fastened, so they work loose over time.

How to tell good work from bad

Ask what underlayment is specified, by product name, not just "underlayment included." Consistent tile alignment and coursing. Ridge and hip tiles mechanically fastened, not just bedded.

Lifespan50+ yrs (tile itself)
Biggest cost driverUnderlayment grade + structure
Common failureUnderlayment fails long before the tile
